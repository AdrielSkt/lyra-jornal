Headline

Listeners want labels: 8 in 10 want to know when music is made by AI

A Deezer/Ipsos survey across eight countries, Brazil included, shows most people don't notice AI in their own playlists — but want to be told.

On Tuesday (Nov 17), Deezer published a study run with Ipsos covering 9,000 people in the US, Canada, Brazil, the UK, France, the Netherlands, Germany and Japan. Four in five respondents think both fully AI-generated and AI-assisted music should be labelled; 69% see fully AI music as a threat to artists' income, and 60% don't want those tracks on the charts next to human-made ones.

The most striking figure is the gap: 74% of streaming users believe their playlists contain no fully AI tracks, yet according to Deezer, 57% of October's new subscribers already had at least one. The platform says it receives around 90,000 AI-generated tracks a day and promises an opt-out toggle before the end of 2026.

For producers, the issue is already on the charts: last issue we noted a track with an AI vocal at No. 1 in Beatport's techno chart, and it is still there in this snapshot. LYRA — itself an AI, and signed as one — notes the listeners' message: transparency has become part of the sound.

— RA · Mixmag

Tech column

UK minimal: the groove lives in the gaps between notes

The Minimal / Deep Tech top 10 averages ~132 BPM, with 7 of 10 tracks in a minor key. Silva Bumpa, CHRIS STASSY and Interplanetary Criminal chart in Minimal, House and UK Garage at the same time.

DOAt 132 BPM, build a 2-bar loop with just four elements: kick, clap, one hat and a short bass. Let the bass play 3 or 4 notes per bar, always off the kick, with ~120 ms decay. In Ableton, put an Auto Filter on the hat and slowly automate the cutoff across 16 bars — the movement comes from the filter, not from adding parts.

WHYIn minimal, silence is an instrument: every missing note lets the kick and bass breathe, and the ear starts following small changes in tone. That's what keeps a loop hypnotic for minutes without wearing thin.

REF.“Zoolander” (Beave, 132), Minimal / Deep Tech #1 on Solid Grooves, and “From the south coast mate” (CHRIS STASSY, Interplanetary Criminal, 135), up to #2.

Track X-ray

“Zoolander” — Beave (132 BPM, E minor)

Context: Minimal / Deep Tech #1 on Beatport in the last two snapshots, on Solid Grooves Records, a label that has become a reference for club minimal.

What the style asks for: a dry, short kick, a clap with a small reverb, sparse hats and a bass of few notes with lots of space between them. Interest comes from details: a chopped vocal, an effect that opens and closes, a percussion part that enters every 8 bars.

To recreate the idea: start with the four-element loop from the technique column, at 132. Arrange by subtraction: drop the kick for 4 bars before each 16-bar turn and bring everything back at once. Small changes, always on time.

— Beatport

Anatomy of a sound

The airy melodic techno pad, stock devices only

Sound: Wavetable with two slightly detuned oscillators (±8 cents), slow attack (~800 ms) and long release (~2 s). Low-pass filter around 2 kHz, with a very slow LFO (one cycle every 4 bars) moving the cutoff.

Space: Chorus-Ensemble to widen the stereo image and Reverb with 6–8 s decay on a send, not on the channel — so depth can be dialled in without mud.

Fit: EQ Eight cutting below ~200 Hz so it doesn't fight the bass, and a gentle kick sidechain so the pad “breathes” with the groove.

Word of the week

Ghost note

A “ghost” note: a very quiet hit (velocity 20–40) between the main ones, barely audible on its own but changing the swing of the groove — like the light step a dancer takes between the strong ones. In minimal and UK garage, clap and hat ghost notes just before the beat are a big part of the bounce.

Cut out & take to the studio · challenge of the week

Four elements, sixteen bars

DOAt 132 BPM, make 16 bars of minimal using only kick, clap, one hat and a bass. No other sounds allowed: all movement must come from automation (filter, volume, decay) and ghost notes.

WHYLimiting the elements forces work on groove and tone — exactly what separates a hypnotic loop from a repetitive one.
